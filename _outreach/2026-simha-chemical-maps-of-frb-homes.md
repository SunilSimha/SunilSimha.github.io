**TL;DR:** we mapped the chemical make-up, specifically the oxygen content,a of gas across five galaxies that produced radio flashes, and found that it varies so much within each galaxy that a single number per galaxy can give the wrong idea about the place the flash came from.

## Context

Fast radio bursts are flashes of radio waves from other galaxies that last about a thousandth of a second. The leading suspects for what makes them are magnetars: the collapsed cores of dead stars, with the strongest magnetic fields known.
The universe started out with almost nothing but hydrogen and helium. Every heavier element, such as oxygen and iron, was made inside stars. Gas that has been through more generations of stars is richer in those elements.
That matters because the chemical make-up of the gas a star forms from affects how it lives and dies, and so perhaps whether it leaves a magnetar behind. If bursts favour gas that is rich or poor in heavy elements, that is a clue to how they are made.

Until now, astronomers have mostly measured one value for a burst's whole home galaxy. That is like describing a whole city's air quality from a single sensor. What you care about is the air on one particular street.

## What did we do?

We used a new instrument on the Magellan telescopes in Chile called LLAMAS. This is just like the KCWI instrument we used for [the CHIME burst]({% link _outreach/2025-chime-brightest-nearby-flash.md %}). It produces thousands of images of a galaxy at different wavelengths of light, which allows us to map the gas across the galaxy and measure its chemical make-up at every point.

We observed four nearby galaxies that have each produced a burst, and added the existing data from KCWI for the CHIME burst, FRB 20250316A.

## What did we find?

- Within most of these galaxies, the amount of oxygen in the gas changes from place to place by a factor of two or more.
- At four of the five burst locations, the gas has a similar oxygen content to the Sun's, give or take. At the fifth it is lower.
- In all five, the burst happened within several thousand light-years of gas lit up by newly formed stars, although not always in the busiest star-forming part of the galaxy.
- A single measurement of the galaxy can differ from the value at the burst's location, depending on which part of the galaxy the instrument happened to cover.

## Why does it matter?

To learn what kind of star makes an FRB, you want to know the conditions where the burst happened, not the average for a galaxy tens of thousands of light-years across. This study shows that large enough to matter, and that the new instrument can make these maps.
Five galaxies are too few to settle whether bursts prefer a particular kind of gas. The next step is to do the same for many more.

## Words you'll see in the paper

- **Metallicity:** how much of the gas is made of elements heavier than hydrogen and helium. Astronomers call all of those "metals", including oxygen.
- **Host galaxy:** the galaxy a burst came from.
- **Integral-field spectroscopy (IFU):** splitting light into its colours at every point across a galaxy at once.
- **Longslit:** the ordinary kind of instrument, which covers one narrow strip.
- **LLAMAS:** the name of the new instrument.
